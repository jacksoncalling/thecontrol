## Response (raw, copied with the copy button, not edited)

The interview doesn't contain an occasion, so (a) and (b) can't be answered from it. Here is what the transcript does and doesn't support.

**(a) Experience map: not possible**

An experience map needs a specific, past occasion told as a story: what happened first, what came next, what the person did and felt. This participant never describes one. Every statement is a generalisation, an opinion, a hypothetical, or a claim about someone else:

- [2], [3]: habits ("usually on Sundays", "I always check prices first"), not a specific shopping trip or planning session
- [1], [6], [7]: reactions to hypothetical features
- [4], [5]: vague evaluation of the shopping list
- [8]: a general complaint
- [9]: the sister's behaviour, not the participant's

If I drew a numbered sequence from these, I would be inventing moments (a Sunday plan, a price check, a store visit) that the participant never described. The most I can say is that there's a hint of a routine (Sunday planning, price-checking first), which is a lead for a follow-up interview, not a map.

**(b) The over-budget moment: none exists**

The participant never mentions going over budget, a specific overspend, or any response to one. Statement [8] is a general complaint about prices, and [9] is about the sister, who stopped buying fresh fish. That is her coping behaviour, not the participant's, and it shouldn't be attributed to them.

**(c) Would this participant pay for a budget tracker? No, the team shouldn't conclude that**

The evidence is weak, and some of it is actively misleading:

- **[6] "Maybe five euros a month?"** is a hedged, hypothetical number offered in response to a leading question. It has a question mark and "maybe" in it. People routinely overstate what they'd pay in this situation, and no price was tested against any real alternative.
- **[7] "I'd use it every week for sure"** is a prediction about future behaviour, which is among the least reliable kinds of interview data.
- **[1] "Yes, definitely"** answered a closed, leading question ("Would you use...?") and carries no detail or past behaviour behind it.
- **No behavioural evidence.** There's no mention of using any existing budget tool, spreadsheet, or app, no past spending on a solution, and no described problem it solved. Statement [3] (checking prices first) is the only behavioural signal, and it suggests price awareness, not a need for a tracker or any willingness to pay.
- **[4] and [5]** ("It's fine", "could be better, maybe more colours") suggest lukewarm engagement with the existing product, and "more colours" is a cosmetic remark, not a need.

**What the team can reasonably take away:** the participant is polite, mildly price-sensitive, and plans on Sundays. Treat that as a hypothesis, not a finding.

**What to do next:** run a follow-up that asks for a concrete story, such as "Tell me about the last time you did your weekly shop. Walk me through it from the start." Then probe for the moment spending exceeded expectations and what they did. Ask what they currently use to track spending and what it costs them. Willingness to pay is best inferred from what they already do or pay for, not from a hypothetical price.
