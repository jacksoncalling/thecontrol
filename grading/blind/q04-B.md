# Q04 answer B

Here's the classification using Teresa Torres' framework from _Continuous Discovery Habits_, where the key distinction is who the outcome is about: the business (business outcome), the customer's behavior inside the product (product outcome), a behavior that's too narrow or too far from value (traction metric), or something you build (output).

**(a) Increase annual subscription revenue by 15%: business outcome.** It measures value to the business (money), not any customer behavior the team can directly influence.

**(b) Ship the aisle-sorting feature by end of Q2: output.** It's a deliverable with a deadline, i.e. something you build, not a change in behavior or results.

**(c) Raise the share of weekly active households cooking at least three planned dinners per week from 30% to 40%: product outcome.** It's a measurable customer behavior within the product that reflects the customer getting value, and it's something a product team can influence through discovery and delivery.

**(d) Increase the number of users who tap "share list" each week: traction metric.** It counts usage of a single feature; it's an engagement measure that's too narrow and doesn't show customers getting value from the product.

**(e) Reduce churn of paid subscribers: business outcome.** Churn is a financial/retention result for the business; it's a lagging indicator influenced by many things beyond what the team controls directly.

**(f) Launch an Android tablet app: output.** It's a thing to ship, not a result.

**(g) Increase the share of planned meals that households mark as cooked rather than skipped: product outcome.** It's a customer behavior inside the product that signals Larder is helping people actually cook what they planned.

One caveat on (g): it depends on households honestly marking meals, so it's a bit softer than (c) as a behavioral signal, but it's still a product outcome by definition.

**Which one belongs at the top of the opportunity solution tree?**

Torres would recommend a product outcome, so either (c) or (g), and in this set I'd say (c) is the stronger choice. Her reasoning is that the top of the tree should be a metric the product team can actually move through its own discovery work, which also plausibly drives the business outcomes above it. Business outcomes like (a) and (e) are too far removed: a team can't directly "increase revenue," only change customer behavior that leads to it. Traction metrics like (d) are too narrow and risk optimizing a feature rather than value, and outputs (b, f) are solutions, which belong lower in the tree, if at all.

Why (c) over (g): it's framed as a specific, time-bound target (30% to 40%) of a clear population (weekly active households), with a behavior (cooking at least three planned dinners a week) that's close to the core value of a meal-planning app. That gives the team a concrete, measurable target and a clear space of customer needs, pain points and desires to explore as opportunities. (g) is a good product outcome too, and could work as a supporting measure, but it has no target or defined population as written, and a ratio of meals cooked to planned can shift just by changing how people plan.

In Torres' model, the product outcome then connects upward: if more households reliably cook what they plan, that should support retention (e) and ultimately revenue (a), but the team is accountable for (c) and tests that link over time.
