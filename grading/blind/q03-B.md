# Q03 answer B

**Verdict: weak.** Almost nothing in this transcript is usable evidence of what the customer actually does. Only statement [2] comes close to behavior, and it's a generalization rather than a story.

**Why it's weak, question by question**

1. **"Would you use a feature that suggests meals based on your weekly budget?"** is a hypothetical, closed question that pitches the solution. Torres is explicit that people are poor predictors of their future behavior and that "would you" questions yield opinions, not evidence. It also leads: the answer [1] is a polite yes to an idea the interviewer clearly likes. It tells you nothing about whether this person has ever tried to solve the problem.
    
2. **"How do you usually plan your meals?"** asks for a generalization ("usually"). Torres wants a specific, recent instance ("tell me about the last time you..."). Answers [2] and [3] are self-reported summaries ("I'm pretty organised", "I always check prices first"). "Always" is a red flag: nobody always does anything, and there's no way to check it against a real episode. The interviewer also didn't follow up on either answer.
    
3. **"What do you think about our shopping list?"** asks for an opinion about your product, not a story about their life. [4] and [5] are vague and polite ("it's fine", "maybe more colours"). Opinions on features invite feature-request noise, and the interviewer let the vagueness stand without asking when the person last used the list or what happened.
    
4. **"If we added a budget tracker, how much would you pay?"** is another hypothetical, now with a price anchor. Statements [6] and [7] ("maybe five euros", "I'd use it every week for sure") are exactly the kind of unreliable predictions Torres warns about. They shouldn't inform pricing or prioritization.
    
5. **"What's the most frustrating thing about groceries in general?"** is the closest to a good topic, but "in general" again produces abstraction. [8] is a complaint everyone shares. [9] is hearsay about the interviewer's customer's sister, which isn't the customer's own experience at all. The interviewer didn't dig into either.
    
6. **The close ("Great, thanks, that's really helpful")** praises answers that weren't helpful, and the interview never collected a single story.
    

**The pattern overall:** every question was either hypothetical, opinion-seeking, or generalizing. No follow-up questions were asked ("what happened next?", "walk me through that"), so there were no timelines, no context, no workarounds, no needs, pain points, or desires to extract for an opportunity map. The interviewer also confirmed their own ideas (budget meals, budget tracker) rather than discovering the customer's world.

**Rewrite of the first question**

Torres' approach starts with a story prompt about a specific, recent episode, with no solution mentioned:

> "Tell me about the last time you planned your meals for the week. Where were you, and what did you do first?"

Then follow with prompts that keep them in the story: "What happened next?", "How did you decide what to buy?", "At what point did you look at prices, and what did you do with that?" If budget matters to them, it will surface through their actual behavior, and you can ask "tell me about a time the budget got in the way" without ever pitching the feature.
