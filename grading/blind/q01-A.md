# Q01 answer A

**Definitions used (Torres):** An opportunity is an unmet need, pain point, or desire in the customer's life, expressed from their perspective. A solution is a specific thing we could build. Customers often state needs as solutions ("I need a button that…"), so we look for the need underneath.

## (a) Opportunities

|#|Opportunity (customer's voice)|
|---|---|
|1|Not a clear opportunity on its own (see c).|
|2|"I waste food because I forget what's in my fridge and it goes off before I use it."|
|3|"I can't start planning from what I already have, so I end up with recipes that need things I don't own."|
|4|"I can't plan dinners without knowing which nights my partner will be home, and I don't find out in time."|
|5, 6|"When I learn my partner's availability late, I have to redo the plan." (Same need as 4, showing the consequence.)|
|7|"Shopping takes too long because I walk back and forth between aisles."|

Statements 2 and 3 are closely linked: 2 is the pain (food waste), 3 is the specific gap in the planning flow. In an opportunity solution tree, 3 could be a child of 2.

Statements 4 to 6 are one opportunity: coordinating household availability with planning.

## (b) Solutions presented as needs

- **8** ("a button that sorts the list by aisle"): the likely opportunity is 7, shopping takes too long because of the backtracking. Sorting by aisle is one way to address it; others could be a store-layout-aware list, grouping by category, or something else.
- **10** ("sync with my partner's work calendar"): the likely opportunity is 4/5/6, not knowing in time when the partner is home. Calendar sync is one solution. The excerpt suggests alternatives, such as a quick "who's home?" prompt to the partner. Note the interviewee's actual problem was a slow reply (two hours), and their partner's schedule is only "sometimes" late on specific days, so a calendar may not even capture it.
- **11** ("I'd probably pay for it if it did all that"): this is not a feature request but a purchase-intent statement. It is neither a need nor a solution, and it is a weak signal (see c).

## (c) Not confidently classifiable

- **1:** This is context-setting (when, where, with what). It shows the flyer is part of her process, which could hint at an opportunity such as "I plan around what's on sale." But she doesn't say that. _To find out:_ ask how the flyer influences her choices and what she does with it.
- **9** ("I usually give up halfway through planning anyway, everybody does"): This hints at a real opportunity (planning is too effortful to finish), but it is a generalization about "everybody" rather than a specific story. It isn't grounded in a recent instance. _To find out:_ ask for the last time she gave up halfway, what stage she was at, and what made her stop. Don't treat "everybody does" as evidence.
- **11:** As above, willingness to pay is hypothetical ("probably", "if it did all that"). It doesn't tell us which part matters. _To find out:_ ask what she currently pays or does to solve this and what she has tried before.
- **12** ("the leftovers are the real problem for us"): This is a potentially important opportunity, possibly more important than anything else she said, but the interviewer didn't follow up. It is unclear whether "leftovers" means cooking too much, not eating what's left over, or throwing it away (which would connect to statement 2). _To find out:_ ask for a specific recent story about leftovers: what happened, what she did, and why it was a problem.

**Overall note:** Statement 12 is a reminder to the interviewer to follow up. Her last remark redirects the conversation toward what she herself says is the real problem, and the interview ended without exploring it.
