Context: Larder is a meal-planning and grocery-list app for households (invented product). Below is an excerpt from a customer interview. Statements are numbered.

Interviewer: Tell me about the last time you planned your meals for the week.
[1] Last Sunday, around four, I sat down at the kitchen table with Larder open and the supermarket flyer next to me.
[2] I started with the fridge, because the week before I'd thrown out half a bag of spinach and a pack of chicken that had gone off.
[3] I scrolled the recipes, but I couldn't find any way to start from what I already had, so I typed "spinach" into search and got about forty results, most of them needing things I didn't have.
Interviewer: What happened next?
[4] I gave up on that part and texted my partner to ask which nights he'd be home, because on Tuesdays and Thursdays he sometimes works late and then nobody eats the dinner I planned.
[5] He answered two hours later, and by then I'd already finished the list.
[6] So I had to redo Thursday.
Interviewer: And the shopping itself?
[7] On Monday in the shop the list was in alphabetical order, so I kept walking back and forth between the aisles; it took me almost an hour.
[8] You really need a button that sorts the list by aisle.
[9] Honestly, I usually give up halfway through planning anyway, everybody does.
[10] It would be great if Larder synced with my partner's work calendar.
Interviewer: Anything else?
[11] I'd probably pay for it if it did all that.
[12] Anyway, the leftovers are the real problem for us.

Using Teresa Torres' Continuous Discovery definitions:
(a) List the statement numbers that contain an opportunity, and phrase each opportunity in one short line from the customer's point of view.
(b) List the statement numbers that are solutions (feature requests) presented as if they were needs. For each, name the opportunity that most likely sits behind it, if the excerpt shows one.
(c) For any statement you cannot classify confidently, say so and say what you would need to find out.

## Response (raw, copied with the copy button, not edited)

No product outcome has been defined yet, so I haven’t triaged these against one. The classification below holds regardless, but relevance and priority depend on the outcome. Which customer behavior is the Larder team trying to change right now?

### (a) Opportunities

- **[2]** “I buy food and then throw it out because I didn’t plan around what I already had.” (spinach and chicken the week before)
- **[3]** “I can’t start planning from what’s already in my fridge, so search gives me recipes that need things I don’t have.”
- **[4], [5], [6]** (one opportunity): “I don’t know which nights my partner will be home while I’m planning, so I finish the plan, then have to redo it, and dinners go uneaten.”
- **[7]** “In the shop, my list doesn’t match how the store is laid out, so I walk back and forth and shopping takes almost an hour.”

[2] and [3] are close. [2] is the consequence and [3] is the friction in the planning flow. If they become siblings in the tree, make sure they don’t overlap. Alternatively, [2] could be the parent and [3] a child.

### (b) Solutions presented as needs

- **[8]** “A button that sorts the list by aisle.” Behind it is **[7]**: the list order doesn’t match the shopping route. Sorting by aisle is only one way to address that.
- **[10]** “Sync with my partner’s work calendar.” Behind it is most likely **[4]–[6]**: not knowing household availability when planning. The excerpt suggests a calendar may not even fix it, since the partner “sometimes” works late, which sounds irregular. The need is knowing who’s eating when, and there are many ways to address that.

### (c) Can’t classify confidently

- **[9]** “I usually give up halfway through planning anyway, everybody does.” This is generalized, and “everybody does” is speculation. It could point to a real opportunity (planning takes too much effort) or just a figure of speech. I’d ask: “Tell me about the last time you gave up halfway. What step were you on, and what made you stop?”
- **[11]** “I’d probably pay for it if it did all that.” This is a hypothetical prediction about future behavior, not a past experience, so it isn’t an opportunity or usable evidence. I’d ask about what they currently pay for, or have paid for, to solve meal planning or shopping.
- **[12]** “The leftovers are the real problem for us.” This is too vague to classify, and it’s unclear whether it’s the same issue as [2] (waste), a consequence of [4] (uneaten dinners), or something different. I’d ask: “Tell me about the last time you had leftovers. What were they, and what happened to them?”
- **[1]** This is only scene-setting, so it isn’t classified as an opportunity.

Overall, statements [1]–[7] are specific, recent story-based accounts (high fidelity), while [8]–[12] shift to opinion and prediction. That’s the part of the interview where the interviewer’s follow-ups stopped asking for stories.
