## Prompt (pasted exactly, from prompts/q04.txt)

```
Context: Brightfold Labs (invented company) makes Larder, a meal-planning and grocery-list app for households with a free and a paid tier. Here are goals that different people at the company proposed for the Larder product team.

(a) Increase annual subscription revenue by 15%.
(b) Ship the aisle-sorting feature for shopping lists by the end of Q2.
(c) Increase the share of weekly active households who cook at least three planned dinners per week from 30% to 40%.
(d) Increase the number of users who tap the "share list" button each week.
(e) Reduce churn of paid subscribers.
(f) Launch an Android tablet app.
(g) Increase the share of planned meals that households mark as cooked rather than skipped.

Using Teresa Torres' definitions, classify each goal as exactly one of: business outcome, product outcome, traction metric, or output. Give a one-line reason for each. Then say which of these Torres would recommend as the outcome at the top of the team's opportunity solution tree, and why.
```

## Response (raw, copied with the copy button, not edited)

|Goal|Classification|Reason|
|---|---|---|
|(a) Increase annual subscription revenue by 15%|Business outcome|It's a financial result the company needs, but pricing, marketing, and sales all move it, so the product team can't directly control it.|
|(b) Ship aisle-sorting by end of Q2|Output|It's a deliverable (a feature) rather than a change in customer behavior or sentiment.|
|(c) Weekly active households cooking 3+ planned dinners/week, 30% to 40%|Product outcome|It's a specific customer behavior in the product, with a baseline and a target, that product decisions can influence.|
|(d) More users tapping "share list" each week|Traction metric|It counts usage of one feature. It can rise without customers getting more value, and it presumes a particular solution.|
|(e) Reduce churn of paid subscribers|Business outcome|Churn is a business result with many causes (price, life changes, competitors), and it has no stated baseline or target.|
|(f) Launch an Android tablet app|Output|It's a thing to build and ship, not a change in what customers do or feel.|
|(g) Raise the share of planned meals marked cooked rather than skipped|Product outcome|It's a customer behavior in the product that is directly influenceable and measurable, though no baseline or target is given yet.|

**Recommendation: (c) goes at the top of the tree.**

Torres wants the root of an opportunity solution tree to be a product outcome: a customer behavior or sentiment the team can directly influence and measure. That rules out (a) and (e), which are business outcomes, (d), a traction metric, and (b) and (f), which are outputs. That leaves (c) and (g).

I'd pick (c) for four reasons:

- **It's fully specified.** It has a segment (weekly active households), a behavior (cooking planned dinners), a threshold, and a current and target value (30% to 40%). (g) still needs those.
- **It captures the value Larder promises.** Households that follow through on their plans are getting what the app is for. That's also the most plausible route to the business outcomes: better retention (e) and more subscription revenue (a) should follow.
- **It's rich ground for discovery.** Interviews about the last time someone planned a dinner and didn't cook it will surface real opportunities such as schedule changes, missing ingredients, and recipe fit.
- **It keeps the tree to one outcome.** Torres wants one tree per outcome, and (g) is closely related. It works well as a sibling or child opportunity area under (c), or as a second tree if the team splits its focus.

One caution on (c): because its denominator is weekly active households, the share can rise if low-engagement households drop out. The team should watch the absolute count of households alongside the percentage.
